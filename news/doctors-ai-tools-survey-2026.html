OpenEvidence, ChatGPT and Gemini: Which AI Tools 3,000 U.S. Clinicians Use in 2026

Research Oct 30, 2026·Sermo

Which AI tools do doctors and other clinicians actually use at work? Sermo, a verified online community for healthcare professionals, asked its U.S. members and published the answers on October 30 in its report AI in Healthcare 2026. The survey ran from June 22 to September 11, 2026, and covers 3,067 respondents: 1,820 physicians, 1,022 nurse practitioners and physician assistants, and other clinicians.

Almost everyone uses AI, but most started recently

92% of respondents use AI tools in their work. Adoption is young: 55% of AI users have less than a year of experience, and only 14% have more than two years. Most learned on their own: 41% through trial and error and online resources, 32% from colleagues, and only 18% through training at their workplace.

Which tools clinicians use

Asked "Which of the following AI tools do you currently use?" (several answers allowed), AI users named:

OpenEvidence: 63%, a medical AI tool for clinicians that Sermo describes as HIPAA-compliant. ChatGPT: 53%. AI built into electronic health records: 42%. Gemini: 21%. Claude: 18%. Ambient scribes that write visit notes: Abridge 12%, Nuance DAX 11%, Nabla 2%. Perplexity: 4%.

Physicians explore general-purpose chatbots more than nurse practitioners and physician assistants: 25% of physicians use Gemini and 22% use Claude, about twice the rate of the other group (13% and 11%). Practice setting matters too. ChatGPT is used by 49% of hospital clinicians and 60% in independent private practice. Experience widens the mix: among those using AI for over two years, 66% use ChatGPT and 70% OpenEvidence, against 39% and 50% among those with under six months.

Who pays

31% get AI from their employer and 15% have it bundled into their records system at no extra cost. 16% pay entirely out of pocket; in independent private practice that rises to 30%, against 9% in hospitals. 60% say AI's value for the money has grown over the past year.

Checking the answers

The report's headline is about verification. 94% of AI users check the output at least sometimes, but only 34% always verify it against a second source. 12% described spotting a potentially harmful AI error and 11% a near-miss. One neurologist described AI putting the wrong drug, a chemotherapy the patient should not have been on, into colleagues' notes. Documentation is uneven too: 33% routinely note AI use in patient charts, 34% never do. Clinicians who took accredited AI training verify more often: 49% always check, against 31% of those with no training.

Patients use AI as well, and 98% of respondents have at least one worry about it. The top concerns are self-diagnosis and self-medication (80%) and anxiety from misread results (78%). Sermo notes that its sample is not a census of all U.S. clinicians and that the figures are self-reported.

What this means if you are choosing a tool

Clinicians use several tools for different jobs: a specialized medical search for evidence, a general chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude for letters, summaries and explanations, and a scribe for notes. If you are comparing general assistants, see ChatGPT vs Claude; for how another profession made its picks, read our survey of lawyers. Whatever you choose, the doctors' lesson applies: check important answers against a second source.
